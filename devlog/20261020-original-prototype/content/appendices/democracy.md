# Appendix B — Participation, delegation and decision rules

## Smartocracy and credit

**Brad deGraf** should be credited explicitly for Smartocracy. He is listed as a coauthor of the early research, along with **Marko A. Rodriguez, Daniel J. Steinbock, Jennifer H. Watkins, Carlos Gershenson, Johan Bollen and Victor Grey**. See the original 2007 proceedings paper, [*Smartocracy: Social Networks for Collective Decision Making*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Decision_Making_and_Democracy/Smartocracy.pdf), and [Brad deGraf's Planetwork profile](https://www.planetwork.net/people/brad-degraf).

The original architecture combines networks of trust between people and their judgments about proposed solutions. Different methods of aggregating trust and direct votes yield different collective outcomes; these methods need not have identical fairness or accountability properties.

Possible modern extensions: topic-specific delegations, expiration, revocation, direct-vote override, cycle handling, and transparent analysis of concentrated delegated power. Unresolved issues include protecting privacy of the delegation graph, preventing coercion, assessing expertise, and detecting conflicts of interest.

Read more: [Bryan Ford, *A Liquid Perspective on Democratic Choice*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12393); [Two Experiments on Delegation in Voting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09715), which found excessive delegation in the studied conditions.

## Incentives and turnout

Low transaction costs do not guarantee participation. A comparative study of possible systems should consider compulsory participation (with legitimate exemptions), reminders, civic credits, participation rewards, and hypothetical reserves that are reduced for not participating. It matters whether casting a valid abstention or delegating satisfies any obligation. Coercion, unequal financial effects, accessibility and uninformed participation all require evaluation.

See [International IDEA: Compulsory Voting](https://www.idea.int/data-tools/data/voter-turnout-database/compulsory-voting). Australia is an existing example of legally compulsory federal voting; differences among jurisdictions and enforcement rules matter.

## Counting rules are separate from transport

- Plurality / first-past-the-post.
- Two-round runoffs.
- Instant-runoff ranked-choice ballots.
- Approval and score voting.
- Condorcet rules, single transferable vote and proportional representation.
- Budget allocation and participatory planning.

Digital interfaces may help explain, collect, and tally complex ballots, but these rules can also be implemented with paper. Different rules change incentives and can yield different winners; the interface should never silently choose the policy objective.

## The broader system

Observation and measurement → analysis and prediction → deliberation → authorized decision → implementation → public feedback. Digital twins can inform decisions about environmental outcomes; Pol.is, Decidim and vTaiwan illustrate aspects of deliberation and participation; none substitutes for legitimate collective authorization.

References: [Pol.is](https://pol.is/), [Decidim](https://decidim.org/), [vTaiwan](https://info.vtaiwan.tw/).
