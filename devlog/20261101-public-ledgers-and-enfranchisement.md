# November 1, 2026 — Public ledgers and the question of belonging

Anselm shared [*Securing Voter Data Systems* (2019)](https://identitywoman.net/wp-content/uploads/SecuringVoterDataReport_Vescent_June2019.pdf) by Heather Vescent, Kaliya Young and Juan Caballero. A [reading note in Appendix E](/appendix-community-voting.html#learning-from-voter-data-systems) records the report's scope and its relevance to the project.

This prompted a useful clarification of what “digital voting” means in the wider argument. The imagined direction is a shared public ledger, perhaps with a social tapestry of relationships and attestations helping communities recognize participants. Anselm has not proposed a formal rule for who gets to vote. We should keep that question open rather than silently choosing a reputation score, endorsement threshold, identity provider or blockchain.

The distinction is between recognizing a person, establishing their standing in a particular decision, and verifying a recorded ballot and count. A social connection is not automatically an entitlement; an eligibility credential is not a tally proof. A public record should make the process inspectable without demanding that a participant's identity, relationships or choices be exposed.

This gives the next experiments a clearer purpose. Can a community recognize legitimate participants while including newcomers and resisting fabricated identities or colluding endorsers? Can someone discover and appeal exclusion? Can private credentials separate admission from ballot submission? Who can preserve and independently verify the record if one operator disappears?

The essay now states this wider ambition, and the existing community-voting appendix carries the questions and citation. The app still uses attest.monster sign-in and an operator-hosted signed record. This entry makes no change to eligibility, voting rules, infrastructure or the implementation's security claims.
